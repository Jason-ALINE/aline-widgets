Thermal Movement

When machinery heats up during operation, it expands. That expansion shifts the shaft centerline — so machines that are perfectly aligned cold may be misaligned when running. Thermal movement calculations let you set the correct cold position from the start.

Understanding Thermal Growth

Why machines move when they heat up

When machinery is operating, the moving parts create friction that builds heat, causing the equipment to expand. This expansion — called Thermal Growth — moves the shaft centerline vertically as the machine rises on its feet.

The amount of movement can be predicted when you know the machinery's material, the temperature change from ambient to operating, and the distance between the shims and the shaft centerline.

Thermal Growth Formula
Growth = α × L × ΔT
α = coefficient of expansion (material)
L = height from shims to shaft centerline
ΔT = temperature rise from ambient to operating

Setting the correct cold position

Most machinery must be misaligned cold so that the shafts will be collinear during normal operating conditions. The graph shows a typical example — the pump stays at ambient while the motor rises as it reaches operating temperature.

Pump stays the same · Motor rises .004" at both feet · Motor must be set .004" low when cold
Example: Motor estimated to rise .004" when at operating temperature. Set the motor shaft .004" lower than the pump while cold — it rises into alignment when running.
Key Principle

Misalign cold to align hot

A cold zero-zero alignment is rarely the correct target. Calculate thermal growth first, offset your cold alignment target by that amount, and your shafts will be collinear at operating temperature.

After accounting for thermal growth in the vertical move, always recheck the horizontal move — vertical machine movement can affect horizontal readings.
